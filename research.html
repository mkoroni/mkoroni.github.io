Research

Modelling and inversion of seismic wave propagation

The research combines seismic observations with numerical wave propagation, full-waveform modelling and inversion. Emphasis is placed on understanding how seismic waveforms respond to complex Earth structure, from mantle discontinuities and the core–mantle boundary to sedimentary basins, surface topography and regional ground motion.

A common thread is the use of physics-based simulations to determine where waveform information originates, how sensitivity is distributed within the Earth, and how seismic observations can be used to constrain volumetric structure and boundary geometry.

−

Full-waveform modelling and inversion

Full seismic waveforms contain information about wave propagation, structural heterogeneity and internal interfaces that is not fully captured by simplified ray-theoretical measurements.

Spectral-element simulations, adjoint methods and iterative inversion are used to analyse this information while accounting for the finite-frequency character of seismic waves.

Waveform-based measurements and Fréchet sensitivity kernels make it possible to determine where seismic observations are sensitive to volumetric parameters and boundary topography. This provides a framework for investigating non-linear effects, parameter trade-offs and the limitations of simplified interpretations.

For global mantle discontinuities, an inversion framework was developed in which full waveforms and adjoint kernels are used to update the geometry of internal boundaries. The broader aim is to formulate inverse problems that account directly for the physics of the complete seismic wavefield.

Mantle discontinuities and precursor sensitivity

Mantle discontinuities, particularly the interfaces near 410 and 660 km depth, provide important constraints on the thermal, mineralogical and dynamical state of the Earth's interior.

The interaction of seismic waves with these boundaries has been investigated using exact synthetic seismograms, spectral-element modelling, reflected seismic phases and adjoint sensitivity kernels.

SS and PP precursor observations were examined to determine how measured traveltime shifts respond to boundary topography, volumetric elastic parameters and finite-frequency wave propagation.

Core–mantle boundary waveforms

Seismic signals that interact with the core–mantle boundary are highly sensitive to heterogeneity and morphology near the boundary and are therefore valuable for probing deep-Earth structure.

By combining full-waveform simulations with phase-specific observations, it is possible to identify which parts of the signal carry information about boundary geometry and which are dominated by other structures along the path.

The approach is useful for testing the resolving power of different seismic phases and for quantifying how uncertainty in one structural component propagates into inferences about the deep mantle.

Surface topography and basin effects

Local site response is shaped by the interaction between shallow sediments, topography and the underlying regional crustal structure. These effects are most pronounced in valleys and mountain settings, where 3D geometry and basin depth strongly modulate shaking.

In the Swiss Alps and similar settings, the relationship between basin structure, surface topography and seismic amplification is investigated using high-resolution 3D simulations and dense observational networks.

These studies aim to identify which features of the near-surface geology and valley geometry are most important in governing amplitude, duration and polarization of ground motion.

Physics-based ground motion and seismic hazard

Large-scale numerical simulations provide a direct route to connect crustal structure to the spatial variation of shaking. This is particularly relevant for areas where the geologic setting is complex and standard empirical ground-motion models may not fully capture the controlling physics.

My work extends from forward wave propagation to the interpretation of observed ground motions, with a focus on identifying the structural drivers of amplitude variability and predicting realistic shaking patterns across regions.

The goal is not only to simulate strong motions, but to determine which features of the Earth need to be known for robust hazard assessment and which simplifications are scientifically justifiable.

Related work

  • Physics-based ground-motion modelling in the Swiss Alps and active tectonic regions
  • Regional simulation workflows using high-performance computing resources
  • Coupling of crustal structure, site effects and probabilistic seismic hazard assessment
