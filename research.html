Research

Modelling and inversion of seismic wave propagation

The research combines seismic observations with numerical wave propagation, full-waveform modelling and inversion. Emphasis is placed on understanding how seismic waveforms respond to complex Earth structure, from mantle discontinuities and the core–mantle boundary to sedimentary basins, surface topography and regional ground motion.

A common thread is the use of physics-based simulations to determine where waveform information originates, how sensitivity is distributed within the Earth, and how seismic observations can be used to constrain volumetric structure and boundary geometry.

Full-waveform modelling and inversion

Full seismic waveforms contain information about wave propagation, structural heterogeneity and internal interfaces that is not fully captured by simplified ray-theoretical measurements.

Spectral-element simulations, adjoint methods and iterative inversion are used to analyse this information while accounting for the finite-frequency character of seismic waves. My work connects this approach to the challenge of understanding how crustal structure modifies earthquake shaking and how that knowledge improves prediction.

Waveform-based measurements and Fréchet sensitivity kernels make it possible to determine where seismic observations are sensitive to volumetric parameters and boundary topography. This provides a framework for investigating non-linear effects, parameter trade-offs and the limitations of simplified interpretations.

Mantle discontinuities and precursor sensitivity

Mantle discontinuities, particularly the interfaces near 410 and 660 km depth, provide important constraints on the thermal, mineralogical and dynamical state of Earth's interior.

The interaction of seismic waves with these boundaries has been investigated using exact synthetic seismograms, spectral-element modelling, reflected seismic phases and adjoint sensitivity kernels.

SS and PP precursor observations were examined to determine how measured traveltime shifts respond to boundary topography, volumetric elastic structure and finite-frequency wave propagation. The resulting kernels show that precursor measurements are sensitive to spatially extended regions rather than to an infinitesimally thin ray path.

Core–mantle boundary waveforms

The core–mantle boundary is a major physical interface separating the solid mantle from the liquid outer core. Its structure is relevant to the thermal and dynamical evolution of the planet.

Imaging this region is challenging because seismic phases interacting with the boundary are affected simultaneously by lower-mantle velocity variations, boundary topography and finite-frequency propagation.

Spectral-element simulations and adjoint sensitivity analysis have been used to study reflected, refracted and diffracted P- and S-wave phases related to the core–mantle boundary. The results demonstrate the importance of considering velocity variations and boundary topography together.

Ground-motion modelling in complex geometries

Physics-based simulations are used to investigate earthquake ground motion at regional and local scales, with particular attention to the influence of complex geological structure and surface morphology on seismic wave propagation.

Current research focuses on three-dimensional sedimentary basins, irregular basin geometry, basin-edge effects, pronounced surface topography and the interaction between low-velocity subsurface structures and surrounding relief. In the Rhône Valley, the spectral-element method is used to simulate seismic wave propagation through a complex Alpine environment.

Comparisons between model configurations provide insight into amplification, waveform duration and spatial patterns of ground motion produced by complex geological and topographic structure. This is the bridge between crustal imaging and ground-motion prediction.

Generative waveform modelling and broadband prediction

Physics-based simulations are highly informative, but they are computationally demanding and limited by the frequency band they can resolve within a practical runtime. Generative waveform modelling provides a complementary route for synthesising realistic broadband seismic waveforms that retain the physical structure learned from simulation-based data.

This line of work combines full-waveform and physics-based simulations with modern generative models to learn the statistical structure of high-frequency wavefields while conditioning on physically meaningful inputs. The objective is not to replace simulation, but to extend the predictive value of physics-based modelling beyond the explicit frequency range of direct numerical runs.

In a regional or local setting, this allows efficient generation of realistic seismic waveforms and improved training data for next-generation ground-motion models. The approach is particularly relevant for broadband hazard workflows, where the aim is to retain physically interpretable structure while recovering the variability needed for engineering applications.

Numerical methods and high-performance computing

Large-scale waveform simulations and inversions require efficient numerical methods, parallel computing and substantial computational resources.

High-performance computing forms a cross-cutting component of the research, supporting both global simulations of the full Earth and higher-frequency regional simulations with detailed geometries. The computational workflows include spectral-element wave-propagation codes, adjoint calculations, iterative optimisation, seismic data processing, parallel simulation pipelines and scientific visualisation.

These methods make it possible to represent internal boundaries, sedimentary structures, topography, complex computational meshes and large three-dimensional model domains within physically consistent numerical simulations.
