Research Overview

Crustal structure and earthquake shaking

My research bridges seismic imaging and ground-motion science to answer a fundamental question: which features of the Earth's crust determine how the ground shakes during an earthquake? Using controlled full-waveform simulations, sensitivity analysis, and targeted inversions in two contrasting tectonic regions (central Japan and the Swiss Alps), I establish which crustal structures redistribute seismic energy and when knowledge of that structure improves shaking predictions.

This work connects two fields that have developed largely in parallel: seismic tomography, which images the Earth's interior, and ground-motion science, which measures surface shaking. The frontier lies in showing how images from one field improve predictions in the other.

Deep crustal drivers of ground motion

Central hypothesis: Crustal structures—including velocity anomalies, faults, and the crust–mantle boundary—leave measurable, reproducible imprints on earthquake shaking that can be detected and distinguished from source effects.

The crust is heterogeneous at all scales: thermal anomalies create velocity variations; faults and geological contacts create velocity contrasts; deep interfaces scatter and redirect waves. Yet distinguishing which of these features actually controls observed shaking, rather than simply varying in correlation with it, requires controlled experiments.

In Japan, I use dense recordings from the national seismic networks (K-NET, KiK-net) and detailed regional tomographic models to test how mid-crustal anomalies (magma chambers, thermal anomalies) alter recorded waveforms and ground-motion measures. Using a hierarchy of models—from layered crust to full 3D structure—I measure the contribution of each structural feature while holding the source fixed. Targeted full-waveform inversion then sharpens anomalies identified as important, and the refined model is tested on withheld earthquake records.

Shallow and deep structure coupling

Question: When does deeper crustal structure control a site's seismic response? How much of the observed variation in ground motion is due to local geology, and how much to the waves arriving at the site shaped by deeper propagation?

Site response—the amplification or de-amplification of shaking by the shallow subsurface—is traditionally modeled in isolation: seismic waves are prescribed at a reference rock depth and their interaction with overlying sediments is calculated. This one-dimensional approach assumes the waves arrive vertically from below. In reality, the 3D crust determines the direction, timing, and amplitude of wave arrival; sedimentary basins and surface topography then modify that 3D wavefield further.

In the Swiss Alps, I use high-resolution crustal models and dense station networks to test whether placing the same shallow geology (sediments, topography) above different crustal structures produces different ground-motion responses. I compare predictions from fully 3D simulations with those from 1D site-response calculations. Where differences are large, targeted inversion improves the crustal model. The advance is to identify when deep-Earth knowledge is necessary for accurate site-response prediction—and when simpler 1D methods suffice.

Sensitivity kernels and structural imaging

Methodological foundation: Finite-frequency sensitivity kernels show which parts of the Earth a given seismic observation is sensitive to, accounting for the finite wavelength of seismic waves.

Classical ray theory predicts that a seismic phase is sensitive only to the thin ray path it traces through the Earth. In reality, seismic waves have finite wavelength and interact with structure over a larger region. Sensitivity kernels—computed using adjoint methods—map this region. They reveal that a single seismic measurement (e.g., a waveform or traveltime) can be sensitive to structure across a wide area, and that sensitivity depends on frequency.

I have developed and applied adjoint sensitivity kernels to understand how seismic waves sense mantle discontinuities, core-mantle boundary structure, and crustal heterogeneity. These kernels are invaluable for: (1) inverting for structure by computing gradients for iterative improvement; (2) understanding why two different Earth models can produce similar waveforms (a phenomenon called non-uniqueness); and (3) designing networks and choosing frequencies to maximize sensitivity to target structures.

Generative models and broadband waveform synthesis

Emerging direction: High-frequency earthquake waveforms can be generated using machine learning models trained on physics-based simulations, extending predictions beyond the frequency limits of direct simulation.

Physics-based simulations using spectral-element methods (SEM) are accurate and physically interpretable, but computationally expensive. They are currently limited to frequencies of roughly 1–10 Hz for regional domains. Engineering applications require shaking predictions up to 10–25 Hz or higher. Hybrid methods combine low-frequency SEM simulations with statistical or machine-learning models for higher frequencies.

I co-developed a generative machine-learning model (denoising diffusion probabilistic models, or diffusion models) that learns to generate high-frequency waveforms conditioned on low-frequency physics-based simulations. The model is trained on thousands of recorded earthquakes and SEM predictions, and can synthesize realistic broadband waveforms (0.5–25 Hz) that are both physically grounded and statistically accurate. Current work extends this to the Swiss region and integrates it with full 3D ground-motion simulations.

High-performance computing and regional simulations

Technical foundation: Large-scale 3D wave-propagation simulations require specialized numerical methods, parallel computing, and optimized workflows.

Simulating seismic wave propagation across a 200 km × 200 km × 100 km region at frequencies up to 10 Hz requires: (1) a high-resolution computational mesh with tens of millions of elements; (2) time-stepping of millions of steps; (3) parallel algorithms on modern GPUs and CPUs. The Swiss Seismological Service has invested in these tools through allocations on CSCS (Swiss National Supercomputing Center), enabling simulations that were impossible a decade ago.

I lead the coordination of SED's access to CSCS resources for regional ground-motion simulation. I have developed and optimized workflows for: building and validating 3D meshes; running coupled SEM simulations; interfacing with domain-reduction methods (DRM) for nested simulations; and post-processing results. These tools now support multiple research projects, including ENSI-funded ground-motion hazard assessment for Swiss nuclear power plants.

Research Vision

The IMPULSE project

My current research direction is formalized in the IMPULSE project: a systematic investigation of how crustal structure controls ground motion across two tectonic regions, from small well-recorded earthquakes to large uncertain ruptures. IMPULSE asks: which crustal features must we resolve to predict shaking, at what scales, and under what conditions does that knowledge remain predictive? The outcome will be a physical framework linking Earth-imaging and earthquake-prediction science.
