Research

Modelling and inversion of seismic wave propagation

The research combines seismic observations with numerical wave propagation, full-waveform modelling and inversion. Emphasis is placed on understanding how seismic waveforms respond to complex Earth structure, from mantle discontinuities and the core–mantle boundary to sedimentary basins, surface topography and regional ground motion.

A common thread is the use of physics-based simulations to determine where waveform information originates, how sensitivity is distributed within the Earth, and how seismic observations can be used to constrain volumetric structure and boundary geometry.

− 01

Full-waveform modelling and inversion

Full seismic waveforms contain information about wave propagation, structural heterogeneity and internal interfaces that is not fully captured by simplified ray-theoretical measurements.

Spectral-element simulations, adjoint methods and iterative inversion are used to analyse this information while accounting for the finite-frequency character of seismic waves.

Waveform-based measurements and Fréchet sensitivity kernels make it possible to determine where seismic observations are sensitive to volumetric parameters and boundary topography. This provides a framework for investigating non-linear effects, parameter trade-offs and the limitations of simplified interpretations.

For global mantle discontinuities, an inversion framework was developed in which full waveforms and adjoint kernels are used to update the geometry of internal boundaries. The broader aim is to formulate inverse problems that account directly for the physics of the complete seismic wavefield.

Mantle discontinuities and precursor sensitivity

Mantle discontinuities, particularly the interfaces near 410 and 660 km depth, provide important constraints on the thermal, mineralogical and dynamical state of Earth's interior.

The interaction of seismic waves with these boundaries has been investigated using exact synthetic seismograms, spectral-element modelling, reflected seismic phases and adjoint sensitivity kernels.

SS and PP precursor observations were examined to determine how measured traveltime shifts respond to boundary topography, volumetric elastic structure and finite-frequency wave propagation.

The resulting kernels show that precursor measurements are sensitive to spatially extended regions rather than to an infinitesimally thin ray path. This finite-frequency behaviour influences the interpretation of discontinuity topography and the spatial resolution of precursor-based measurements.

These studies also demonstrated that the relationship between precursor measurements and discontinuity structure can be strongly non-linear, highlighting the limitations of ray theory when used in isolation.

Core–mantle boundary waveforms

The core–mantle boundary is a major physical interface separating the solid mantle from the liquid outer core. Its structure is relevant to the thermal and dynamical evolution of the planet.

Imaging this region is challenging because seismic phases interacting with the boundary are affected simultaneously by lower-mantle velocity variations, boundary topography and finite-frequency propagation.

Spectral-element simulations and adjoint sensitivity analysis have been used to study reflected, refracted and diffracted P- and S-wave phases related to the core–mantle boundary.

Ray-theoretical predictions were compared with measurements made directly on full-waveform synthetic seismograms. The sensitivity of individual phases to volumetric structure and boundary geometry was then evaluated using adjoint kernels.

The results demonstrate the importance of considering velocity variations and boundary topography together and support the use of complementary seismic phases within waveform-based inversion strategies.

Ground-motion modelling in complex geometries

Physics-based simulations are used to investigate earthquake ground motion at regional and local scales, with particular attention to the influence of complex geological structure and surface morphology on seismic wave propagation.

Current research focuses on three-dimensional sedimentary basins, irregular basin geometry, basin-edge effects, pronounced surface topography and the interaction between low-velocity subsurface structures and surrounding relief.

In the Rhône Valley, the spectral-element method is used to simulate seismic wave propagation through a complex Alpine environment. The numerical models include three-dimensional basin structure, realistic material-property variations, irregular subsurface interfaces and true surface topography.

A series of model configurations is considered to separate the influence of basin sediments, basin geometry, topographic relief and their combined interaction.

Comparisons between these configurations provide insight into amplification, waveform duration and spatial patterns of ground motion produced by complex geological and topographic structure.

This research extends earlier investigations of boundary geometry and finite-frequency wave propagation toward regional-scale problems in which several interfaces, strong material contrasts and complex geometries interact within the same numerical model.

Numerical methods and high-performance computing

Large-scale waveform simulations and inversions require efficient numerical methods, parallel computing and substantial computational resources.

High-performance computing forms a cross-cutting component of the research, supporting both global simulations of the full Earth and higher-frequency regional simulations with detailed geometries.

The computational workflows include spectral-element wave-propagation codes, adjoint calculations, iterative optimisation, seismic data processing, parallel simulation pipelines and scientific visualisation.

These methods make it possible to represent internal boundaries, sedimentary structures, topography, complex computational meshes and large three-dimensional model domains within physically consistent numerical simulations.
